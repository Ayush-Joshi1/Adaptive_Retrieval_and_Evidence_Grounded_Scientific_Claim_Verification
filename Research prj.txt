# 📄 Paper 1 — RAGAS

**RAGAS: Automated Evaluation of Retrieval Augmented Generation**

The paper introduces RAGAS as a framework for evaluating RAG systems without requiring reference answers for every evaluation. It focuses on multiple dimensions of a RAG pipeline, including whether retrieved contexts are relevant and whether generated answers are faithful to those contexts. ([arXiv][1])

### Why this matters to us

Remember our original question:

> Does retrieval depth affect factual reliability?

We need to measure **two different things**:

```text
                 RAG SYSTEM
                     │
          ┌──────────┴──────────┐
          │                     │
       RETRIEVAL             GENERATION
          │                     │
          ▼                     ▼
  Was useful context       Did the answer
     retrieved?             stay faithful?
```

This is important because imagine:

```text
K = 5
```

produces a bad answer.

We can't immediately conclude:

> "Retrieval got worse."

Maybe retrieval was excellent, but the LLM **failed to use the retrieved information**.

That's why separating retrieval and generation evaluation is useful.

### Research lesson #1

> **A RAG system has multiple components, so an overall answer score doesn't necessarily tell us which component caused the result.**

That is a VERY important idea for our experiment.

---

# 📄 Paper 2 — ARES

**ARES: An Automated Evaluation Framework for Retrieval-Augmented Generation Systems**

ARES evaluates RAG systems along three dimensions:

* **Context relevance**
* **Answer faithfulness**
* **Answer relevance**

It uses lightweight language-model judges and a small amount of human-annotated data to improve evaluation reliability. The paper evaluates across multiple knowledge-intensive tasks and datasets. ([arXiv][2])

This gives us another useful insight.

Suppose our experiment produces:

| K  | Context relevance | Faithfulness |
| -- | ----------------: | -----------: |
| 1  |              high |         high |
| 5  |              high |         high |
| 10 |             lower |        lower |

That tells a much more interesting story than simply:

> "K=10 had lower accuracy."

We can potentially investigate **why** performance changed.

### Research lesson #2

> **Good evaluation should help diagnose the system, not merely rank systems.**

---

# 📄 Paper 3 — Lost in the Middle

This one is particularly interesting for our hypothesis.

The paper **Lost in the Middle: How Language Models Use Long Contexts** investigates how language models use information placed at different positions within longer contexts.

The researchers found that models can perform substantially worse when relevant information appears in the **middle** of a long context, with performance often being better when relevant information occurs near the beginning or end. ([arXiv][3])

Why do we care?

Because our hypothesis says:

> More retrieved documents may eventually become harmful.

This paper gives us a **plausible mechanism** for why additional context could cause problems.

But careful:

### This paper does NOT prove our hypothesis.

Their experiment is about information placement in long contexts, not exactly our Top-K RAG experiment.

That's an important research distinction.

We can say:

> "Previous research demonstrates that models may struggle to effectively use information in longer contexts."

We **cannot** say:

> "Therefore K=10 will definitely reduce our RAG accuracy."

We haven't tested that.

### Research lesson #3

**Related evidence is not the same as evidence for our exact hypothesis.**

That's research thinking.

---

# 📄 Paper 4 — RAGTruth

RAGTruth focuses on hallucination in RAG systems.

The authors created a corpus containing nearly **18,000 naturally generated RAG responses**, with manual annotations at case and word levels for hallucination analysis. ([arXiv][4])

Why is this relevant?

Because our phrase:

> **"factual reliability"**

needs an operational definition.

We can't just throw around "reliability."

We need to decide what we actually mean.

One possibility is:

> **Does the generated answer make claims that are supported by the retrieved context?**

That's closely related to **faithfulness / hallucination**.

## Day 1 → Step 2: Lock the research design

Before we open VS Code, we're going to turn what we learned from the papers into a **precise experiment**.

The literature supports treating RAG as more than just "did the final answer look good?" RAGAS separates retrieval/context quality from how faithfully the LLM uses that context, while ARES evaluates context relevance, answer faithfulness, and answer relevance as distinct dimensions. ([arXiv][1])

So here's the design I recommend.

---

# 1. Our final research question

### **RQ**

> **How does retrieval depth affect the factual reliability of answers generated by a Retrieval-Augmented Generation system?**

Where **retrieval depth = K**, the number of documents/chunks passed to the LLM.

This is narrow enough for 4 days and still gives us a meaningful empirical investigation.

---

# 2. Our hypothesis

### **H1**

> **Increasing retrieval depth will initially improve answer reliability by providing additional relevant evidence, but excessive retrieval depth may decrease reliability because additional context can introduce irrelevant or distracting information.**

And we should also have a **null hypothesis**.

### **H0**

> **Changing retrieval depth has no systematic effect on answer reliability under the experimental conditions.**

Why bother with H0?

Because we're researchers, not fortune tellers. 😂

We need to allow our experiment to show that our prediction was wrong.

---

# 3. What exactly are we changing?

### Independent variable

**Retrieval depth K**

Our initial conditions:

```text
K = 1
K = 3
K = 5
K = 10
```

So for every question:

```text
Question
   │
   ├── K=1  → 1 chunk → LLM → answer
   │
   ├── K=3  → 3 chunks → LLM → answer
   │
   ├── K=5  → 5 chunks → LLM → answer
   │
   └── K=10 → 10 chunks → LLM → answer
```

Everything else stays the same.

---

# 4. What are we measuring?

Here's where I want to make an important adjustment.

We **shouldn't try to measure 10 things**.

For a 4-day project, I'd use **three core measurements**.

### Metric 1 — Answer correctness

**Did the system give the correct answer?**

This is our primary outcome.

For a question like:

> Who developed the theory of relativity?

Expected:

> Albert Einstein.

If the generated answer says something factually incorrect, that's a failure.

---

### Metric 2 — Faithfulness

**Is the generated answer actually supported by the retrieved context?**

Example:

Retrieved context:

> Einstein published the special theory of relativity in 1905.

Answer:

> Einstein published the special theory of relativity in 1905.

✅ Faithful.

But:

Retrieved context:

> Einstein published the special theory of relativity in 1905.

Answer:

> Einstein published special relativity in 1905 and won the Nobel Prize in 1921 for this theory.

The second claim may not be supported by that retrieved context.

So:

**Correctness and faithfulness aren't identical.**

That's exactly why RAG evaluation literature treats these dimensions separately. ([arXiv][1])

---

### Metric 3 — Retrieval relevance

This answers:

> **Did the retriever actually bring useful information?**

Because suppose:

```text
K=1
```

retrieves one highly relevant document.

But:

```text
K=10
```

retrieves that same useful document + nine unrelated documents.

Then K=10 isn't necessarily a better retrieval result simply because it retrieved more information.

This is extremely important to our research question.

# 6. One BIG decision: dataset

This is our next major decision.

We need a dataset containing questions where we can determine whether answers are correct.

We have several possible directions:

### Option A — Existing RAG benchmark

Use an established QA dataset.

**Pros:**

* Research credibility
* Existing questions/answers
* Easier evaluation

**Cons:**

* Some datasets can be complicated
* Dataset preprocessing may take time

### Option B — Build a small controlled dataset ourselves

For example, collect a set of factual questions from a defined document collection.

**Pros:**

* Extremely controlled
* Easy to understand
* Fast

**Cons:**

* More risk of introducing our own bias
* Less standardized

### Option C — Use an established knowledge-intensive benchmark

Potentially something from the RAG evaluation literature.

This gives us stronger research grounding, but we need to make sure it's manageable within four days.

# 9. One more important thing

I don't want to blindly install RAGAS and say:

> "RAGAS gave us 0.83, therefore our system is 83% reliable."

That's not good research.

Automated RAG evaluation itself has limitations. RAGAS and ARES are useful references for how to evaluate different dimensions, but we still need to understand **what our metric actually means** and how we're applying it. RAGAS specifically proposes reference-free evaluation across multiple RAG dimensions. ([arXiv][1])

So we'll decide exactly how evaluation works **before** running the final experiment.
